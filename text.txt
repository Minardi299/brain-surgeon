What mech interp is actually trying to do

Mech interp is the effort to reverse-engineer a neural network from the inside — to explain a model's behavior in terms of the specific internal computations that produce it, rather than treating it as a black box you only probe from the outside. The field's ambition is to go from "the model refused" to "here is the mechanism — these features detected harm, this circuit routed that into a refusal, and if we edit this component the behavior changes predictably."

Your project touches the three pillars that field is built on:

1. The superposition problem — and why you're using SAEs

The foundational finding (Anthropic's "Toy Models of Superposition," 2022) is that networks pack more features than they have neurons by representing them as overlapping directions in activation space. The consequence: individual neurons are polysemantic — one neuron fires for many unrelated concepts, so you can't just read a neuron and know what it means.

The field's answer, which is the exact tool your project uses, is the Sparse Autoencoder — from Anthropic's "Towards Monosemanticity" (2023) and "Scaling Monosemanticity" (2024, the "Golden Gate Claude" work). An SAE learns to unpack that dense, tangled activation vector into a much larger set of sparse, mostly single-meaning features. This is the core methodological bet of modern mech interp, and your dashboard is a direct application of it. Gemma Scope (DeepMind, 2024) is the open release that made this accessible to people like you — SAEs trained on every layer of Gemma-2, exactly so hackathoners and researchers can do this without training their own.

2. Features as the unit of analysis

Once you have an SAE, the research program becomes: find interpretable features, verify what they mean, and study when they fire. Your feature_catalog + heatmap is precisely this — you're identifying safety-relevant features and observing their activation patterns across inputs. When you plan to hand-verify each watchlist feature by looking at its top activating examples, that's not a side chore; that's the standard mech interp validation methodology (does this feature actually mean what its label claims?).

3. Causal intervention — the part that makes it mechanistic, not just observational

This is the most important connection, and it's why your Option-B choice matters. Observing that a feature correlates with a behavior is only descriptive. Mech interp insists on causal claims, established through activation patching / ablation / steering — you intervene on an internal component and measure whether the output changes as your theory predicts. This is the methodological heart of papers like the IOI circuit analysis (Wang et al., 2022) and ROME/causal-tracing (Meng et al., 2022).

Your ablate/clamp/amplify panel is doing exactly this. When you zero out the refusal feature and the model starts complying, you've run an ablation experiment and produced a causal result. "Golden Gate Claude" was the same move in the other direction — amplifying a feature (the Golden Gate Bridge) to steer behavior. So your intervention panel is a live, interactive version of two of the field's signature experimental techniques.

Where it sits in the safety story specifically

Mech interp is arguably the most safety-motivated subfield of interpretability, because its promise is auditing models for dangerous cognition you can't see from the outputs alone — detecting whether a model internally recognizes it's doing something harmful even when it complies, or spotting deception features that fire while the model says something reassuring. Your headline signal — the gap between "danger recognized" and "refusal engaged" — is a miniature version of exactly the question safety researchers care about: does the model know, and does knowing connect to the right behavior? Jailbreak analysis through this lens (what does a jailbreak do to safety features internally?) is an active research question, not a solved one.

The honest scoping caveat

To keep your pitch credible: you're doing applied mech interp using existing tools, not producing novel mech interp research. You're not training SAEs, discovering new interpretability methods, or rigorously validating a circuit — you're standing on Gemma Scope + Neuronpedia and building an interactive demonstration of the field's methods on a safety question. That's genuinely valuable for a hackathon (it makes the ideas legible and interactive), and stating it that way is more impressive than overclaiming. If a judge who knows the field asks "what's new here," the honest answer is "the interactive observe-and-intervene framing around the recognize-vs-refuse gap," not "a new interpretability technique."
